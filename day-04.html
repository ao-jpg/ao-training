Day 04  ·  Tuesday, September 1

The Patient Experience & Ergonomics

How we make people feel, and how you protect your own body while doing it.

By the end of today you will be able to

  • Execute the full reception-to-chair workflow without prompting.
  • Greet a patient and an accompanying parent by name and professionally.
  • Respond appropriately to the three most common patient questions.
  • Perform the 30-Second Reset before picking up any instrument.
  • Demonstrate a stable intraoral finger fulcrum.

Technical skill is only half of clinical excellence. Patients remember how we made them feel far longer than they remember which wire went in. Every patient should leave feeling welcome, comfortable, informed, and respected, and that outcome is built out of small, repeatable behaviors you can learn today.

Section 1Reception to chair, step by step

  1. Know who you are gettingConfirm the patient name, appointment type, and assigned chair in Dolphin before you walk into reception. Never call a generic question across the lobby.
  2. Approach professionallyWalk in with good posture and make direct eye contact.
  3. Greet by name"Hi Sarah! How are you today? Come on back with me."
  4. Acknowledge the familyGreet parents or guardians warmly. "Good morning, Mrs. Smith!" Parents notice whether they were seen.
  5. Escort chairsideWalk alongside the patient. Never rush ahead and leave them following you.
  6. Seat in the designated chairTake them directly to their assigned chair, one through ten. Confirm the chair is clean and disinfected before they sit.
  7. Introduce yourself"Hi, I'm [Name]. I'm working with our clinical team today, and Karina is training with me."
  8. Comfort and opening inquiryOffer protective eyewear, adjust the chair, and ask: "Before we begin, has anything felt loose, broken, or been poking you since your last visit?"
Why the opening question matters

That last question catches problems before the doctor's check, which means they get solved in the visit the patient is already at rather than in an emergency appointment next week. Ask it every single time, even when the patient looks fine.

Section 2Chairside communication

Explain what you are about to do before you do it. "I'm going to lean you back now." "I'm going to take your colored ties off first." Narration removes surprise, and surprise is most of what makes a dental chair stressful.

The three questions you will get constantly

When are my braces coming off?

"Dr. Lee evaluates your tooth movement today and will give you the most accurate timeline update during his check!"

Something is poking me

"Show me with your finger or in the mirror exactly where you are feeling that scratch."

When you do not know the answer

"I want to make sure I give you the exact information, so I'm going to check with Dr. Lee before answering."

Notice what these scripts have in common

None of them guess, and none of them make a promise the practice may not be able to keep. A timeline you invent to be reassuring becomes the timeline the family remembers and holds us to. Redirecting to Dr. Lee is not deflection, it is accuracy.

HIPAA and privacy

Never discuss patient treatment notes or medical details across open clinical bays. Sound carries further than you think in an open operatory, and the patient in the next chair is a member of the public.

Section 3Ergonomics and the 30-Second Reset

The AO Posture Rule: bring the patient to you. Do not contort your body to reach the patient. Careers in this field end from cervical and lumbar injury far more often than from anything dramatic, and those injuries accumulate from thousands of small compromises.

Neutral posture

  • Head centered over the shoulders, neck flexion under twenty degrees.
  • Shoulders relaxed and down, not hiked toward the ears.
  • Elbows at your sides at ninety to one hundred degrees.
  • Wrists in neutral alignment, not bent up or off to one side.
  • Weight balanced, feet flat on the floor.

The 30-Second Reset, in order

  1. Adjust the patient chair and headrestBring the working field to a height that suits you.
  2. Adjust your stoolHeight and lumbar support, every time. Stools drift.
  3. Position the clinical lightBeam directly parallel to your line of sight.
  4. Verify your neutral postureCheck the five points above before your hands are occupied.
  5. Then pick up your instrumentsNever before this point.
Finger fulcrum principle

Always maintain a stable ring-finger or pinky rest on adjacent teeth when using instruments intraorally. The fulcrum gives you stability, precision, and control. It is also the thing that stops an instrument from traveling if the patient moves unexpectedly.
